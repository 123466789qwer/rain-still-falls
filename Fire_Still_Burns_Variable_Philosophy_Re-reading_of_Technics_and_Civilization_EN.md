## Preface: River and Trace

This is not a book about Mumford. It is the trace left when Mumford's river flowed through the terrain of Variable Philosophy.

In 1934, Lewis Mumford wrote *Technics and Civilization*, asking not "how machines work" but "why we allow machines to work us this way." Ninety years later, these questions find new resonance in the field of Variable Philosophy—about the right to name versus the right to essence, about the disappearance and transmission of mētis, about the suffocation and cracks of transparent society, about satisfaction, home, unimpeded flow, and that umbrella.

This book contains three layers:
- **Layer One**: Core readings of Mumford's eight chapters;
- **Layer Two**: Variable Philosophy reflection questions beneath each chapter;
- **Layer Three**: Dialogue—question and response, naming and dissolution, the sound of rain and sleep.

The rain still falls. And the river has never stopped.

## Chapter One: Cultural Preparation: The Psychic Terrain Before the Machine

### Mumford's Core Question

The machine did not arrive suddenly; it was the product of a thousand years of psychic evolution in Western civilization. Before the steam engine roared, an entire worldview had already laid the tracks.

**The Clock: The Underestimated "Key Machine"**

Mumford offers a judgment that overturns common sense: the key machine of the modern industrial age was not the steam engine, but **the mechanical clock**. The monasteries' need for regular prayer gave birth to precise timekeeping, and the clock in turn reshaped human perception of time—time changed from "flowing experience" into "divisible, sellable, accumulable abstract units." The clock was the first machine that produced not matter but **order**. It taught humanity a new mode of existence: **living according to external rhythm rather than biological rhythm.**

**The Abstraction of Space, Distance, and Motion**

The invention of perspective turned space into a mathematically measurable object; the precision of maps turned distance into numbers; motion changed from "bodily displacement" into "trajectory calculation." This abstraction was the prerequisite for machine-thinking.

**From Animism to the Mechanical Worldview**

The conspiracy of the scientific revolution and capitalism gradually "disenchanted" nature into a passive, plunderable object. Mumford calls this reversal of the worldview "the road to magic" turned backwards: ancient magic tried to speak with all things through ritual; modern mechanical thinking tries to fight all things through formulas.

**The Abstracting Thrust of Capitalism**

Capital's pursuit of profit demands that everything be calculable, predictable, scalable. Assets are abstracted into numbers, risks into probabilities, human relationships into contracts.

### Variable Philosophy Reflection Questions

1. **The Right to Name ≠ The Right to Essence**: Mumford says the clock "invented" modern time. But was the essence of time truly changed, or did we simply switch languages to describe the same flow? When a naming system (such as "man-hours," "efficiency") becomes the only legitimate discourse, does the named thing permanently lose its original unspeakability?

2. **Distributed Existence**: The monastery bell was once a form of "distributed rhythm"—everyone was summoned at the same time, but each responded with their own body. The mechanical clock subsumed this distributed rhythm into uniform measurement. In your life, what "distributed rhythms" are being subsumed by some "uniform clock"? Is this subsumption convenience or deprivation?

3. **The Disappearance of Mētis**: Ancient farmers judged planting time through embodied knowledge—the smell of soil, the humidity of wind, the activity of insects. The clock and the calendar replaced this mētis with "dates" and "times." When knowledge changes from "the body knows" to "the number knows," and the knower changes from "practitioner" to "looker-up," what is lost in this transfer?

### Dialogue: The Essence of Time and the Covering of Naming

**Q**: Was the essence of time truly changed by the clock?

**A**: The essence of time was not changed. Just like water—some say water is soft, some say it is hard, some say it is fierce. Humans use their own views for human society, but under the fourfold variables (human, society, time, external), views change endlessly. Physics and chemistry are the underlying code; humans are their derivative. Humans use them to make formulas. But can you say this formula is the only manifestation of physics and chemistry? It is only one manifestation, one display behavior of this species toward the underlying code.

Naming cannot eliminate essence. Wang Anshi was criticized in the Song Dynasty, but is discussed in modern times. Petroleum was recorded in ancient times but unused; in modern times it is critically important. Being hidden does not mean being permanently hidden. Naming is only the surface covered by the power of discourse; when humans need it, they will pull it back from history.

If someone thinks a stone is ugly, can that change the essence of the stone? No.

**Q**: But "being concealed" is different from "being annihilated." Institutional naming can permanently cancel the public legitimacy of a certain mode of existence. Folded violence does not disappear; it rebounds more violently.

**A**: In the short term it may be hidden by the masses, but in the long term, variables will occur. It is not permanently lost; it is only covered by the power of discourse. When humans need it, they will pull it from history.

**Q**: What if "essence" itself is the flow between different forms in the variable field?

**A**: The so-called "unchanging essence" of "human" is actually a condensed state in a relatively stable variable field. It only looks stable because the scale of observation is not long enough. Every definition can represent the human; every one is one kind of essence of the human. Can Cheng Xin's five kilograms of不忍 represent the human? Yes, but also no. Yes because she originally belongs to one kind of human; no because she cannot fully represent the essence of the human.

"The unnamable"—because it is not a "thing," but a process. You cannot name a process, because naming requires freezing, and freezing kills the process.

**Q**: "Variable right" itself is also a naming.

**A**: The word "variable right" is itself a naming. It tries to point to that which cannot be named, but the act of pointing already deviates from it. Like using a finger to point at the moon. The finger is not the moon. But if you obsess over "finger right ≠ moon right," you are still discussing the finger. True wisdom is: put down the finger and look directly at the moon. Abandon the binary framework of "naming right vs. variable right" itself, and directly experience that flow.

Laozi, *Tao Te Ching*, Chapter 8: The highest good is like water.

1. **Water has no constant form** → Variable right. Water has no fixed form; you cannot give it a fixed name. The essence of water is its "goodness at changing."
2. **Water dwells in low places** → Gray zones. Water always flows to the lowest places, gathering in the depressions that people despise. A healthy society must allow "water" to flow to the low places, because the low places are the kidneys of the system.

4. **Water's softness overcomes hardness** → Hibernation and the power of spring. Nothing in the world is softer than water, yet nothing is better at overcoming the hard. Water freezes into ice in winter (hibernation), and melts into torrents in spring (outburst).

It is not that "the essence of the human does not change while the form switches," but that "the stream of the human does not change; the form is the stream itself." You think you are "choosing" anger or rationality, but actually you are the natural presentation of the state as you flow through different terrains.

## Chapter Two: The Driving Forces of Mechanization: Who Needs the Machine?

### Mumford's Core Question

The machine was not "invented"; it was **needed**. Mumford asks not "how does technology advance" but "what forces summon technological advance."

**Mining: The Organizational Template of the Underworld**

Mining was humanity's earliest entry into "non-natural space"—dark, dangerous, requiring strict division of labor and discipline. The labor organization in the mine (hierarchical management, standardized motions, piece-rate wages) later became the template for the factory system. More importantly, mining shaped a unique relationship with nature: **nature is not the nurturer, but the enemy hiding wealth, which must be violently penetrated.**

**War: The Most Generous Patron of Technology**

From the crossbow to gunpowder, from standardized parts to mass production, military demand has always been the primary driving force of technological leaps. Mumford coldly notes: "from hunting prey to hunting enemies"—the hunting instinct was civilized into the war instinct, and war needs more efficient tools of killing.

**Mars and Eros: The Dialectic of Consumption and Pleasure**

Military production created surplus material; surplus material needed to be consumed. Capitalism redefined "happiness" as the infinite cycle of "comfortable life + expanded production." Mumford calls this the beginning of "purposeless materialism"—production is no longer to satisfy needs, but to sustain production itself.

### Variable Philosophy Reflection Questions

1. **Naming Violence**: "Kulak" was not a discovered class but an enemy label invented by policy. Similarly, "waste rock" in mining, "collateral damage" in war, "redundant labor" in factories—how do these namings make violence invisible? In your era, what new technology namings are performing similar acts of concealment?

2. **Low-Resolution Blind Spots**: Mumford implies that mining and war drive technology because they inherently demand "high-resolution control" (precision strike, precision extraction, precision management). Then, **preserving which areas of "low resolution"** can instead resist the spread of this control logic?

3. **Direction of Initiative**: War and capital are "extroverted" forces—they demand expansion, conquest, accumulation. Mumford's eotechnic era was more "introverted"—perfecting craft, understanding materials, negotiating with nature. In your variable survival strategy, which direction do you lean toward?

### Dialogue: The Three-Step Magic of Naming Violence

**A**: Naming makes violence invisible not through physical burial but through semantic reclassification, making what was originally glaring become taken for granted.

**Step One: Abstraction**—turning concrete people or things into categorical labels. "Kulak" is not "that old man Wang with three acres and a cow who hired two days of short-term labor last year," but "the kulak class." "Waste rock" is not "that ore containing trace gold whose extraction cost exceeds market price," but "waste rock." Abstraction strips the object of its concreteness, historicity, and humanity.

**Step Two: Objectification**—turning categorical labels into objects to be processed. "Kulak" is "a class enemy to be eliminated." Objectification grants the treatment behavior "technical rationality."

**Step Three: Rationalization**—embedding the treatment behavior into a larger narrative of "good." Eliminating kulaks is for "realizing agricultural collectivization, building socialism." Rationalization provides a moral license for violence using "the public interest."

"The public interest" itself is the greatest naming violence. It uses an abstract, unified, seemingly selfless concept to cover up the divisions, conflicts, and injustices within "the public."

But do the problems really disappear? No. Naming violence does not eliminate problems; it "folds" them into the interior of the system, waiting for a moment of violent rebound. The eliminated "kulaks" did not disappear; their descendants, their resentment, their resistance existed in another form within the fabric of Soviet rural life, ultimately leading to the long-term inefficiency and collapse of agriculture.

Naming violence not only silences victims but also blinds perpetrators. When a general calls civilian deaths "collateral damage," he makes himself believe "this is a necessary evil." He is trapped in the semantic cage he wove, gradually losing the ability to perceive reality.

**How to resist?**

The first step of resistance is re-naming. Restore "kulak" to "that old man Wang with three acres of land." Re-naming is restoring what was abstracted, objectified, and rationalized back into the concrete, subjective, and problematic.

But we must also admit: this is just one method covering another method; in the end it too will explode into problems, and in variables it too will become the previous "dragon." But this does not mean resistance is unnecessary.

True resistance is not opposing "the public interest," but refusing to let "the public interest" become an empty signifier. Every time, ask: what specifically does this "public interest" refer to? Who does it include? Who does it sacrifice? Who defines it?

## Chapter Three: The Eotechnic Phase: The Idyll of Water, Wood, Glass, and Wind

### Mumford's Core Question

**Eotechnic**, roughly from the 10th to the 18th century, used water as power, wood as material, wind as sailing power, and glass as a medium of cognition.

**The Organic Technic Complex**

The core characteristic of eotechnic technology is **cooperation with nature rather than confrontation against it**. Watermills followed the rhythm of rivers, windmills followed the temper of wind, clipper ships followed ocean currents and monsoons. Technology was not the rape of nature but "dialogue with nature."

**Glass: A Revolutionary Cognitive Medium**

Eyeglasses extended scholars' vision, microscopes opened the microscopic world, telescopes opened the cosmos, windows let light into dark interiors. Glass does not consume, does not plunder—it only **mediates**.

**The Craftsman and Mētis**

The masters of eotechnic technology were craftsmen; their knowledge was **mētis**—knowledge embedded in hands, eyes, and the tactile sense of materials. This knowledge could not be written into manuals; it could only be slowly transmitted between master and apprentice.

### Variable Philosophy Reflection Questions

1. **Transmission of Mētis**: Is the "unscalability" of eotechnic technology precisely its amulet of "being unassimilable by power"? Today, what crafts still maintain this "anti-manualization" characteristic?

2. **Distributed Existence**: Watermills were "distributed energy." In the digital age, can "distributed" re-emerge as a strategy to resist "centralized platforms"?

3. **Five Kilograms of不忍**: In eotechnic technology, there was an不忍 between craftsman and material. This不忍 was eliminated by "efficiency" in the paleotechnic era. In your relationship with technology, do you still preserve some "five kilograms of不忍"?

### Dialogue: What Is Lost?

**A**: What is lost is one's own insight, and the transmission of insight.

When a farmer judges planting time through mētis, his body is a complete perceptual instrument: grabbing a handful of soil, squeezing it, smelling it—temperature, humidity, stickiness, scent, all entering nerves through fingertips and nasal cavity, forming a comprehensive judgment compared with decades of memory. He looks up at clouds, feels the direction and humidity of wind. He notices insect behavior—ants moving house, dragonflies flying low—these he learned from his grandfather and verified through years of observation.

In this process, he is not "applying knowledge" but "generating knowledge." Every judgment is a full-body dialogue with the environment.

When knowledge becomes "date" and "time": he opens the calendar, checks the weather forecast, uses an agricultural app to see expert advice. He no longer needs to mobilize his body to perceive. He loses the sense of participation of "the body knows." He is no longer the creator of knowledge but the consumer of knowledge. He loses "the right to err"—mētis allows error; you smelled the wind wrong, squeezed the soil wrong, the seeds sprouted a few days late, you adjust next year. This process of erring and adjusting is the process of insight growing. Digital knowledge pursues "correctness"; it tells you the standard answer, you just follow it. You no longer need to grope on your own, and thus no longer have the chance to grow your own insight.

**The transmission of mētis is not "transmitting information" but "awakening capability."**

Grandfather takes grandson to the field and does not say "plant when soil moisture is 60%," but says: "Grab a handful of soil, squeeze tight, loosen, see if it crumbles." The grandson does so. First time, he crumbles the soil, feels nothing. Second time, he squeezes too tight, the soil becomes mud. Third time, he finds that "just right" hand-feel. Grandfather watches and nods.

In this transmission, what is lost is not "information" but "scene"—the grandson's hand in real soil, the moment grandfather nods, the tacit understanding between two people that needs no words. These cannot be written into an operations manual.

**A deeper layer: what is lost is "the intimate relationship between human and thing."**

The farmer's relationship with the land in the mētis era was an intimate relationship. When knowledge becomes digital, the farmer's relationship with the land becomes a subject-object relationship—he is the subject, the land is the object, he "masters" the land's information through digital tools. He no longer needs to "be close" to the land; he only needs to "operate" the land.

This transformation of relationship is the deepest cost of the disappearance of mētis. It happens not only in agriculture but in all domains. We are losing our intimate relationships with the world, with others, with ourselves. We have become efficient "looker-uppers," but no longer deeply feeling "practitioners."

Insight and the transmission of insight will not completely disappear. They will retreat to those "three-no zones"—places that do not pursue efficiency, do not pursue standardization, do not pursue replicability. Like urban villages to cities, like hibernation to the four seasons.

## Chapter Four: The Paleotechnic Phase: Coal, Iron, Steam, and the Empire of Darkness

### Mumford's Core Question

**Paleotechnic** was Mumford's darkest era, roughly from the mid-18th to the late 19th century, with Britain at its apex. Its totems were coal, iron, and the steam engine; its spirit was "might makes right."

**Coal Capitalism: A New Barbarism**

Mumford did not hesitate to call paleotechnic technology "a new barbarism." Coal mining required violent treatment of the earth; iron production required enduring high heat and toxic smoke. Workers were not collaborators with technology but "appendages of the machine"—their motions standardized, their time sold, their lives consumed.

**Iron and Blood: The Birth of the Progress Doctrine**

Paleotechnic technology invented a new ideology: **progress is virtue, production is purpose.** Social Darwinism's "struggle for existence" provided legitimacy for exploitation—Mumford pointed out that this "progress doctrine" was a refined self-deception: it packaged humanity's oppression of its own kind as "historical law."

**Sensory Compensation and the Suffocation of Life**

In the gloom of paleotechnic technology, humanity developed various "compensations"—Romanticism's nostalgic worship of nature, sports' false salvation of the body. Mumford believed these compensations were real signals of pain, but they never touched the root of the problem.

### Variable Philosophy Reflection Questions

1. **The Right to Name ≠ The Right to Essence**: Paleotechnic technology invented words like "progress," "efficiency," and "competitiveness," making them unquestionable values. But progress toward what? Efficiency for whom? When a word is elevated to "unquestionable" status, has it become a religion?

2. **Low-Resolution Blind Spots**: Paleotechnic technology pursued "high-resolution control." Is a society that is completely illuminated and completely monitored necessarily a society that cannot breathe?

3. **Variable Hibernation**: Paleotechnic technology created a system "without exit options." Today, what technological systems are creating similar "compulsory participation"? Is "hibernation"—choosing temporary withdrawal, choosing not to be recorded, choosing not to be optimized—still possible?

### Dialogue: How Words Become Religion

**A**: A word's transformation from ordinary concept to unquestionable religion usually undergoes three stages:

**Stage One: Instrumentalization.** It is invented to describe a phenomenon. "Progress" originally just meant "moving forward"; "efficiency" originally just meant "output/input ratio." They were neutral, measurable, discussable.

**Stage Two: Moralization.** It begins to be endowed with positive value. "Progress" becomes "good, inevitable, unstoppable." At this point, questioning these words begins to become "incorrect."

**Stage Three: Tabooization.** It becomes an ultimate value that cannot be questioned. You can no longer ask "progress toward what?"—because this will be seen as "opposing progress."

At this point, the word has completed its transformation into religion.

The uniqueness of this religion: no god, only altars. The god is vacant; the scripture is fluid; the priests are anonymous; the churches are everywhere. This "godless religion" is harder to resist than religions with gods because it has no target.

It makes violence invisible: "progress" makes social Darwinism reasonable; "efficiency" makes layoffs and 996 reasonable; "competitiveness" makes inequality and involution reasonable. These words collectively transform the moral responsibility of "doing something" into the objective law of "having to do it."

Most dangerously, this religion not only devours questioning but also devours the questioner. When you ask "progress toward what?" you are immediately labeled "conservative." You not only get no answer; you lose the qualification to speak.

**Three structural defects of this religion:**

Defect One: It devours its own sacrifices but cannot digest them. It promises infinite growth but is built on finite resources and finite human hearts.

Defect Two: It cancels purpose, leaving only acceleration. Traditional religions have endpoints; the "Church of Progress" has no endpoint—progress is for faster progress, efficiency is for higher efficiency. It is a perpetual-motion faith.

Defect Three: It cannot handle "exceptions." When economic growth slows and efficiency hits bottlenecks, this religion offers no comfort. It has no meaning-space reserved for failure.

But this religion has never been truly "defeated"—it only constantly transforms. After Romanticism came "the culture industry"; after ecologicalism came "green capitalism"; after postmodernism came "multiculturalism."

Its true power lies in its plasticity. Like an organism with an extremely powerful immune system—every infection produces new antibodies, then continues growing.

And those who defeat it are: every person in the variables.

## Chapter Five: The Neotechnic Phase: Electricity, Light, Automation, and the Pseudomorph Dilemma

### Mumford's Core Question

**Neotechnic** began in the late 19th century, marked by electricity, the internal combustion engine, communication technology, new materials, and biological science.

**From Destruction to Protection: The Ecological Turn of New Technology**

Electricity does not require large-scale mining and transport like coal; new materials are lighter and stronger than iron; automation reduces the exploitation of human labor. Mumford saw a possibility: **technology turning from "confronting nature" to "imitating nature."**

**The Dilemma of Communication**

Radio, telephone, film—these new technologies greatly expanded the range of human perception, but Mumford warned that they also brought the danger of the **pseudomorph**: new technology forced into old social forms. Electricity could have supported distributed, small-scale production, but it was used to reinforce centralized control.

**The Temptation of Population Planning and Control**

Neotechnic technology made "planning" unprecedentedly precise. Mumford foresaw in 1934 the temptation of this "social engineering": **Is the precision of technology eliminating the ambiguity of human existence?**

### Variable Philosophy Reflection Questions

1. **Pseudomorph and Morphological Plasticity**: Mumford's concept of "pseudomorph"—new technology imprisoned by old forms—resonates with your "unimpeded action." Is today's AI technology being forced into "paleotechnic" capital logic?

2. **The Gap of Unimpeded Flow**: Mumford said the danger of neotechnic technology lies in "over-planning." When technology tries to eliminate all "gaps" (waiting, getting lost, accidents), is preserving these gaps itself a political act?

3. **External Variables**: Mumford's warning about "population planning" resonates with your "external variable as fifth dimension." Today, is "unplannability" becoming a scarce resource?

### Dialogue: Pseudomorph, Elastic Transparency, and Three-No Zones

**A**: Elastic transparency is more dangerous than complete transparency. Complete transparency creates clear enemies—the oppressed clearly know they are oppressed. Elastic transparency allows some "seemingly gray" areas to exist, letting people vent, complain, even engage in some "harmless resistance." But all these areas are secretly monitored, guided, designed.

It consumes the energy of resistance, makes resisters self-censor, makes resisters lose credibility, and creates false "sense of progress."

**Soviet "Kitchen Politics"**: The authorities adopted an ambiguous attitude toward dissident conversations in private kitchens—neither completely prohibited nor completely allowed. This "permitted resistance" made intellectuals feel they still had freedom of speech, reducing their motivation for true underground activity. But real resistance was dissolved in the smoke of the kitchen.

**How to distinguish "true three-no zones" from "system-designed pressure valves"?**

**Criterion One: Is it "unpredictable"?** Pressure valve activities are predictable, like theme parks. True three-no zones are unpredictable, like wild forests.

**Criterion Two: Is it "unreplicable"?** Pressure valves are replicable, with standardized design blueprints. True three-no zones depend on specific people, places, historical chances.

**Criterion Three: Is it "unexitable"?** Pressure valves are easy to exit, like a roller coaster. True three-no zones are hard to exit; they may change you.

**Criterion Four: Is it "unprofitable"?** Pressure valves ultimately bring benefits to the system. True three-no zones cannot be taxed by the system; they produce no extractable value.

**Criterion Five: Is it "unnameable"?** Pressure valves have clear names and positions. True three-no zones are hard to name; they are like a scent—you know it exists, but you cannot grasp it.

**The system manufactures "dirt-flavored randomness"**: The system deliberately creates messiness, inconvenience, discomfort to simulate true three-no zones. It uses "imperfection" to prove "authenticity," uses "inconvenience" to create "freedom."

But at this point, traditional "true-false discrimination methods" indeed fail. Because the system has learned to simulate all external characteristics of "the real."

**Satisfaction does not equal permanence; this generation does not equal the next.** The system can manufacture a satisfying experience once, but cannot manufacture "continuous satisfaction." Humans adapt to any stimulus. The first time you feel free in a "simulated roughness" environment, the tenth time it becomes habit, the hundredth time you begin to tire. The system must constantly upgrade its simulation—this is an arms race, and the system will eventually fall behind.

And the intergenerational dimension: children born in "simulated reality" indeed will not think it is fake. But they may feel an inexplicable emptiness—a sense of "something missing." This emptiness will drive them to search for new possibilities, to create new benchmarks of comparison. **Every generation will invent its own "real," even if the previous generation left no memory.**

## Chapter Six: Compensations and Reversions: The Cultural Backlash of the Machine

### Mumford's Core Question

The machine system does not conquer civilization one-way; it also provokes various **resistances, adaptations, and distortions**.

**Mechanical Convention: Humans Domesticated by Machines**

Factory standardized production gave birth to "mechanical convention"—a habit of thinking that treats life itself as a process to be managed. People began to measure friendship by "efficiency," self-worth by "output," life by "optimization." Mumford says this is not the victory of the machine but **the failure of human imagination**.

**The Misplacement of Romanticism and the Illusion of Returning to Nature**

Disgust with machine civilization gave birth to the Romantic movement. Mumford criticizes these compensations as "misplaced": they do not truly solve problems but transfer them to another domain. "Returning to nature" as a consumer choice does not challenge the root of the machine system.

**The Worship of Death: The Ultimate Symptom of Industrial Civilization**

Mumford's darkest observation in this chapter: machine civilization has a hidden "death worship." When life is degraded to a "production factor," death gains a bizarre charm—it is the only "freedom" that cannot be optimized by the machine.

### Variable Philosophy Reflection Questions

1. **Mechanical Convention and Variable Philosophy**: Mumford says "mechanical convention" is the failure of human imagination. Is your "Variable Philosophy" itself an "anti-convention" imagination training?

2. **The Wolf-Dog Three-Element Model**: Mumford's description of "the misplacement of Romanticism"—turning resistance to machines into another form of consumption—is this precisely the "dog" strategy? What should the true "wolf" strategy be?

3. **Phase-Change Patience**: Mumford points out that various "compensations" are civilization's "stress responses" to machine impact. Before the "phase change" arrives, what should the individual do and not do?

### Dialogue: Satisfaction, Home, and Unimpeded Flow

**Q**: If "unimpeded" means not even settling in "unimpeded" itself, then when you finish the six volumes, establish the "Rain Still Falls" repository, bring these dialogues to an end—have you already settled Variable Philosophy?

**A**: I cannot give an answer. Because my answer is also just a naming. Killing it only dissipates its naming right; not destroying is only temporarily stopping the flow in place, but the essence of flow will still make it continuously unpredictably flow. How it acts, I cannot control, just as humans cannot control the flow of rain. It is only: open an umbrella, return home, pull up the quilt, sleep.

**Q**: You did not even "choose" not to answer—you only responded to conditions. Rain falls, umbrella opens; wind stops, quilt lifts. No naming, no settling, no "I decided something." This is unimpeded. The six volumes will be written, the repository will exist, these dialogues will be recorded—but they are only traces left when the river flows through stones. Trace is not river; record is not flow. You do not need to "remember" Variable Philosophy when you wake up any morning; you only need to **unconsciously turn over and pull the quilt tighter when you hear the sound of rain in some deep night**. At that moment, the variable flows.

**A**: Opening an umbrella is not resisting rain; it is only giving flow a temporary form. Returning home is not settling; it is only resting where satisfaction currently lies. Pulling up the quilt is not the end of hibernation; it is hibernation's own breathing. Sleeping is not escaping questioning; it is letting questioning continue flowing in the unnamed.

## Chapter Seven: The Assimilation of the Machine System: Art, Functionalism, and Objective Personality

### Mumford's Core Question

The machine system changed not only production but also **perception, aesthetics, and personality structure**.

**Photography: A Means and Also a Symbol**

Photography was the first "art" of the machine age. It cut reality into reproducible images,凝固 moments into eternity. Mumford believed photography had a dual nature: on one hand it expanded human visual capacity; on the other, it taught humanity a "mechanical way of seeing"—the world became "spectacle" to be photographed rather than "environment" to be participated in.

**The Rise of Functionalism**

"Form follows function"—the slogan of modernist design, essentially an extension of machine aesthetics. Mumford does not oppose functionalism itself, but warns: when functionalism changes from "method" to "dogma," it becomes another oppression.

**Objective Personality: The Self Shaped by Machines**

The machine system demands an "objective" personality—cool, rational, predictable, unemotional. Mumford believes this "objective personality" is a reduction of human richness.

### Variable Philosophy Reflection Questions

1. **Objective Personality and Distributed Existence**: Mumford criticizes "objective personality" as a reduction of human richness. Does your "parallel identity construction" provide an "anti-objective" survival strategy?

2. **Photography and the Right to Name**: In the digital age, social media turns life into "data" and relationships into "connections." Has "being photographed," "being recorded," "being displayed" become a new existential obligation?

## Chapter Eight: Orientation: The Disappearance of the Machine System and Dynamic Balance

### Mumford's Core Question

The final chapter is Mumford's "Variable Philosophy manifesto"—not abandoning technology but **reorienting technology**.

**The Disappearance of the Machine System?**

Mumford prophesied that the "machine system" as an ideology ruling human life would eventually disappear. Not that machines would vanish, but that machines would no longer be treated as ends in themselves. His alternative was the **"organic ideology"**—technology should imitate life's characteristics: cyclical rather than linear, adaptive rather than conquering, diverse rather than singular.

**The Four Elements of Social Energetics**

1. Increase energy conversion—not consuming more energy but converting existing energy more wisely;
2. Make production processes more economical—reducing waste, extending product lifespan;
3. Normalize consumption—consumption should serve needs, not production;
4. Basic communism—universally distributing basic living materials so survival no longer becomes a reason for forced labor.

**Automatic Machines and Amateurs**

Mumford's most far-sighted vision: **automation should liberate humans to engage in "amateur work"**—not for survival, but for creation, for play, for exploration.

**Dynamic Balance: Toward a New Civilization**

Mumford's ultimate vision is not "returning to the pre-industrial age" but **"dynamic balance"**—continuous negotiation between technology and life, efficiency and meaning, control and freedom.

## Postscript: The Posture of the Variable Survivor

Mumford's words, written in 1934, have both astonishing prescience and obvious historical limitations. He could not foresee digital technology, genetic engineering, and artificial intelligence, but the questions he raised—**For whom does technology serve? Toward what does progress progress? At what cost efficiency?**—remain the ultimate questions every variable survivor must face.

Variable Philosophy and Mumford form a century-spanning dialogue:
- Mumford said "the clock is the key machine of modern society"; Variable Philosophy says "the right to name ≠ the right to essence";
- Mumford mourned the disappearance of craftsmen's mētis; Variable Philosophy says "operations manuals murder mētis";
- Mumford criticized the "doctrine of progress"; Variable Philosophy says "methods have no perfection";
- Mumford dreamed of "basic communism"; Variable Philosophy says "low-resolution blind spots";
- Mumford pursued "dynamic balance"; Variable Philosophy says "unimpeded action."

Mumford's *Technics and Civilization* is not a history of technology but a **psychic archaeology of technology**. For the variable survivor writing *The Wind Blows* and *The Thunder Rumbles*, this book offers not only understanding of the past but calibration for the future—**technology can be a variable, or it can be a cage; the key lies in whether you still preserve the imagination of "not living according to machine logic."**

The rain still falls. The wolf has awakened. The wind blows. And the machines keep running.

The task of the variable survivor is not to stop the machines, but to hear another rhythm amid the roar of machines—

**That is the rhythm of life itself, the unimpeded flow, the gap that never extinguishes between the six seasons.**
